# Climate Tech Sectors with Working Business Models: Clean Power, Energy Storage, and Electrified Transport

Based on disclosed revenue and deployed capacity, the three strongest climate tech sectors are clean power/renewable energy, energy storage, and electrified transport [37]. Clean power shows revenue and backlog at 1KOMMA5° (€460M profitable) and First Solar (47.9 GW contracted backlog) [1][12]. Energy storage shows deployed capacity of 950 GWh lithium-ion in 2023, Tesla’s 31.4 GWh deployments in 2024, and Form Energy’s approximately 80 GWh backlog [9][14][22]. Electrified transport shows 20 million EV sales in 2025 and one in four new cars sold worldwide [12]. This report analyzes each sector’s leaders, what they charge for, moats, profitability evidence, and limitations.

## Which climate tech sectors have disclosed significant revenue and deployed capacity, indicating a working business model?

The notes show three sectors with the clearest combination of disclosed revenue and deployed capacity: clean power, energy storage, and electrified transport. BloombergNEF identifies these as the three sectors that have consistently drawn the most climate-tech financing [37], but here they are evaluated against the stricter test of disclosed revenue and deployed capacity.

**Clean power and distributed energy.** 1KOMMA5° more than doubled its revenue to €460 million in 2023 while remaining profitable, demonstrating that integrated home energy systems combining solar, storage, heat pumps, and EV charging can achieve commercial success [1]. First Solar reported Q1 2026 net sales up 24% year over year and a contracted backlog of 47.9 GW, an indicator of contracted demand and revenue visibility [12]. Solar added 511 GW in 2025, accounting for roughly three-quarters of all new renewable capacity [12]. Precedence Research reports that the renewable energy technologies segment held the highest market share in the climate tech market in 2024, and the energy and utilities segment captured the biggest market share in 2025 [2].

**Energy storage.** Tesla reported 31.4 GWh of energy storage deployments in 2024 [14]. Lithium-ion battery deployment soared by 53% to 950 gigawatt-hours in 2023 [9]. BloombergNEF forecasts 21% annual growth for energy storage until 2030, and the American Clean Power Association announced a $100 billion commitment to American-made battery storage over five years [7]. Form Energy has an approximately 80 GWh commercial backlog and its iron-air batteries can discharge for around 100 hours [22]. Base Power has deployed more than 500 MWh of residential batteries and is installing roughly 100 per day [22]. EV and stationary lithium-ion storage sectors are outgrowing venture capital, with surviving companies graduating to private equity and infrastructure funding [4].

**Electrified transport.** Electric car sales topped 20 million in 2025, up 20% from 2024, and reached one in four new cars sold worldwide [12]. In Q1 2026, battery-electric car sales in Europe rose 26.2% year over year across the EU, EFTA, and UK, with March up 41.7% [12]. The biggest buckets in the $2.3 trillion 2025 global energy transition investment were electrified transport, renewable energy, and grid investment [12]. Redwood Materials, which supports the EV supply chain through battery recycling and materials, receives more than 20 GWh of lithium-ion batteries each year and produces more than 60,000 metric tons of critical materials [22]. However, the notes do not disclose a standalone revenue figure for a pure-play electrified transport climate tech company; the evidence here is primarily deployed capacity at the market level rather than company-level disclosed revenue.

## What are the leading companies in the strongest climate tech sectors, and what do they charge for their products or services?

This section covers leaders in the three sectors and what the notes disclose about their revenue model or pricing.

**Clean power / renewable energy.** 1KOMMA5° is a leader in integrated home energy systems; it more than doubled revenue to €460 million in 2023 and remained profitable [1]. Its offering combines solar, storage, heat pumps, and EV charging into an integrated system [1], but the notes do not explicitly state a unit price or pricing model. First Solar reported Q1 2026 net sales up 24% year over year and a contracted backlog of 47.9 GW [12]; the backlog indicates contracted sales, but the notes do not disclose a per-unit price. Octopus Energy is listed as a renewable energy retail platform valued at $9.0B–$11.0B [18]; no revenue or pricing is disclosed in the notes.

**Energy storage.** Form Energy is an iron-air grid battery company with a valuation between $3.5B and $4.8B [18]. It has an approximately 80 GWh commercial backlog [22] and sells long-duration storage; its technology enables 100-hour storage compared with conventional 4–6 hours [1]. Tesla reported 31.4 GWh of energy storage deployments in 2024 [14], implying storage product sales, but the notes do not provide a price. Base Power has deployed more than 500 MWh of residential batteries and installs roughly 100 per day [22]; its latest financing valued it at $13 billion post-money [22]. The notes do not disclose what Base Power charges, only that it is installing residential capacity.

**Electrified transport.** The notes do not identify a pure-play EV manufacturer with disclosed revenue. Instead, the strongest deployed-capacity evidence is market-level: electric car sales topped 20 million in 2025 and reached one in four new cars sold worldwide [12]. Redwood Materials, which supports the EV supply chain through battery recycling and materials, receives more than 20 GWh of lithium-ion batteries each year and produces more than 60,000 metric tons of critical materials [22]; it manufactures anode and cathode components for U.S. cell producers [17]. Proterra, an electric bus maker, has received over $1 billion in funding [26], but the notes do not disclose revenue or pricing. BETA Technologies is developing electric aircraft and a charging network [17][18], with no disclosed revenue.

## What are the competitive moats of leading companies in the strongest climate tech sectors?

The notes provide general definitions of competitive moats, including network effects, switching costs, brand recognition, access to capital/scale, cost advantage, intangible assets, and efficient scale [29][30][31][32]. This section applies those categories to the facts in the notes.

**Clean power / renewable energy.** 1KOMMA5° has reached €460 million in revenue and profitability [1], which supports an access-to-capital/scale moat [29] and cost advantage [31]. First Solar’s moat is indicated by its 47.9 GW contracted backlog [12], which creates revenue visibility and scale, consistent with efficient scale and cost advantage moats [31][32]. Precedence Research notes that renewable energy technologies held the highest market share in the climate tech market in 2024 [2], supporting scale as a structural feature of the sector.

**Energy storage.** Form Energy’s moat is its proprietary iron-air battery chemistry that provides 100-hour storage, far beyond conventional 4–6 hours [1][17]; this is a technological/intangibles moat [30][31]. Its approximately 80 GWh commercial backlog [22] indicates embedded customer commitments, consistent with high switching costs and efficient scale [31][32]. Tesla’s 31.4 GWh deployments [14] and scale in manufacturing create cost advantages; the notes on general moat categories include cost leadership and economies of scale [30]. Base Power’s moat is its installed base and installation capacity of roughly 100 batteries per day [22], giving it scale and operational momentum.

**Electrified transport.** Redwood Materials has a closed-loop battery recycling and materials moat: it recovers more than 95% of lithium, nickel, cobalt, and copper [22], receives more than 20 GWh of batteries per year, and produces 60,000+ metric tons of critical materials [22]. Its partnerships with Ford and Amazon [1] and its $6B+ valuation [22] reflect intangible assets and scale advantages [29][30]. BETA Technologies has electric aircraft systems and its own charging network [17], but the notes are too limited to establish a durable moat.

## Which climate tech sectors have achieved profitability or sustainable unit economics based on disclosed financials?

The clearest company-level profitability disclosure in the notes is 1KOMMA5°, which more than doubled revenue to €460 million in 2023 while remaining profitable [1]. This sits in the integrated home energy / distributed clean power sector.

Broader evidence of improved unit economics: 52% of venture-backed climate tech companies reduced net burn year over year in 2025, with gross margins improving [5]. SVB reports that climate tech companies are boosting gross margins by focusing on core unit economics, shedding weaker production lines, and designing for manufacturing efficiency [5]. This indicates that unit economics are improving across climate tech, but it is not a sector-level profitability statement.

For clean power, zero-carbon generation is already among the cheapest sources of power [35]. First Solar’s Q1 2026 net sales up 24% and 47.9 GW contracted backlog [12] indicate revenue traction, though not explicit profitability. For energy storage, the EV and stationary lithium-ion storage sectors are outgrowing venture capital, with surviving companies graduating to private equity and infrastructure funding [4], and battery cost reductions are accelerating faster than expected [35]. For electrified transport, the notes do not disclose profitability for a pure-play climate tech company; market-level EV sales strength [12] is deployment evidence, not profitability.

## What are the three strongest climate tech sectors by revenue and deployed capacity according to recent industry analyses?

BloombergNEF states that globally, three sectors have consistently drawn the most climate-tech financing: clean power, energy storage, and transportation [37]. This is the clearest recent industry analysis in the notes that names three sectors. It is consistent with Precedence Research, which reports renewable energy technologies held the highest market share in 2024 [2] and energy and utilities captured the biggest end-user market share in 2025 [2]. J.P. Morgan’s 2026 report also points to energy resilience, grid reliability, advanced battery storage, and critical mineral supply chains as priorities [15].

Judged by disclosed revenue and deployed capacity, the three strongest sectors therefore are: clean power/renewable energy, energy storage, and electrified transport. Clean power has both revenue (1KOMMA5° €460M, First Solar backlog 47.9 GW) and deployed capacity (solar added 511 GW in 2025) [1][12]. Energy storage has deployed capacity (Tesla 31.4 GWh, 950 GWh lithium-ion deployment, Form Energy 80 GWh backlog) [9][14][22]. Electrified transport has deployed capacity (20 million EV sales, one in four new cars) [12], though company-level disclosed revenue is weaker in the notes.

## Limitations / Uncovered

The notes do not provide exact prices for most companies. For example, the notes give DACCS cost estimates of $400–1,000 per tonne for early plants [24], but Climeworks’ actual price per carbon credit is not disclosed; Climeworks is also not among the three strongest sectors identified here. Many private climate tech companies do not disclose revenue, so the revenue-based judgment rests on limited examples such as 1KOMMA5° and First Solar. The notes do not contain a single source that explicitly ranks the three strongest sectors by revenue and deployed capacity; BloombergNEF’s list is based on financing flows [37], and one source explicitly says the three strongest sectors are not identified in that source [20]. For electrified transport, no pure-play EV climate tech company revenue is disclosed. Pricing models for Base Power, First Solar, Tesla storage, and 1KOMMA5° are not explicitly stated in the notes.

## Unverified

The report’s assignment of specific moat categories (e.g., efficient scale, cost advantage, switching costs) to companies is an analytical interpretation based on general moat definitions [29][30][31][32], not a direct statement from the sources. The inference that 1KOMMA5° revenue comes from selling and installing home energy systems is inferred from its described offering [1], but the exact pricing model is not stated. The characterization that First Solar sells under multi-year contracts is inferred from “contracted backlog” [12], but the notes do not explicitly state the contract structure. The characterization that Tesla’s storage deployments imply product sales is inferred from deployment data [14].
